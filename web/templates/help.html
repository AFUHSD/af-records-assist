Records Assist, explained, and how to test it

A helper for public records requests. It drafts the Google Vault search from a request, suggests redactions on the exported emails, and a named person approves every step before anything moves.

Python Flask web app Runs on one Mac today Fictional samples only Updated 2026-11-09

A public records request arrives from a parent, a reporter or a lawyer. Someone has to work out what they really want, search the district's Google Workspace for matching emails and files, strip out anything the law says must stay private, and send the rest back. Two of those jobs take real judgment and real time: writing a search that finds everything responsive without drowning in noise, and spotting every student name, medical detail and attorney note in a pile of emails.

Records Assist puts an AI model on those two jobs as a first-draft assistant. It never runs the search itself, never touches Google Vault, and never releases a file. It proposes, a person decides.

Lost on a screen? Turn on Show tips in the top bar. A numbered dot appears beside each control worth knowing; hover one for a sentence on what it does, or press Walk me through in the bar at the bottom to be shown them in order. It stays on until you turn it off.

Ground rules

Four things to know before you click anything

Fictional data only. Until the district decides where record content may go, every request and every email you put in must be made up. The samples built into the app are fictional. Never paste a real request, name or record.

Everything is advisory. Each search plan and each redaction is a suggestion. Nothing is final until a person approves it and types their name on the sign-off page.

Nothing leaves the Mac except model calls. Requests, uploads and release files are saved in a local folder. The only outside traffic is the text you choose to send to a model.

Vault stays with Technology. The app writes search terms for a person to paste into Google Vault. It has no Vault connection and no one testing it needs Vault access.

The parts

Two layers, one place for the AI logic

The repo has an engine and a web app. The engine is a command-line Python script that Luke Allpress built. It holds the two system prompts, the fixed redaction patterns, the Arizona exemption table and the code that talks to each model. The web app is a browser front end on top of it. It imports the engine rather than copying it, so when a prompt or a pattern changes in the engine, the web app uses the new one on its next start.

The five steps

How a request moves through the app

The app is a wizard. Each page opens with one line saying what to do there. A bar pinned to the bottom shows Back, where you are, and the one next thing to do. The step bar at the top stays clickable so you can always jump back. Finishing a step moves you forward on its own.

1Request

Where the request comes in. Paste the text, or upload the email it arrived in as a .eml or .txt file. Fill in who sent it and when, pick which model drafts the plan, and tick the box confirming the request is fictional.

Page
New request, from the home page's "New request" button
Shortcut
"Use the fictional sample request" fills in the band trip request with one click
Then
"Create and draft the search plan" takes 30 to 40 seconds while the model reads it

Why it is built this way

A request holds no student records, so either model can read it safely. The fictional confirmation is a deliberate speed bump so nobody pastes a real one in by habit while the district is still deciding where record content may go.

2Search plan

The AI reads the request and writes a plan: what is really being asked, what the request leaves open, who likely holds the records, a date range, and the exact Gmail and Drive search terms to paste into Google Vault. Then the page asks one question: is the request clear enough to build a good search? The AI suggests an answer. A person picks.

Yes
Shows the Vault terms with Copy buttons and a "Copy handoff for Technology" note. "Approve plan and continue" moves on.
No
Shows a ready-to-send email to the requester with the AI's questions written up, plus Copy and "Open in Gmail" buttons. Paste their reply underneath and the plan is drafted again with their answers.
Redraft
At the bottom, "Draft again with" lets you pick a different model and compare.

Why it is built this way

The model proposes the words of the search. The engine fixes the shape: one OR group of quoted phrases plus the date range, no AND, no exclusions, and any email address the requester did not write is removed. A small model will happily exclude the very word the request is about, and a search that is too narrow silently loses responsive records, which is the worse mistake. Accounts are chosen inside Vault by a person, never by the model.

3Records

Three numbered boxes on one page. First, record the Vault Count that Technology sends back. Saving it opens a cost estimate card: what the AI processing should cost (from the real token counts of calls made so far, or the calibrated sample numbers) plus the $1-per-page policy charge, with a ready-to-send email asking the requester to proceed, narrow, or withdraw. Mark it sent, then record their answer. Second, upload the Vault export as .mbox, .eml or .txt, or paste one email. Third, choose the rig, Claude, or both, and run the redaction check.

Shortcut
"Add the 6 fictional sample emails instead" loads the benchmark emails without any upload
Per record
Each row shows "Not checked" or the number of suggestions and which model found them, with Check and Remove buttons
Then
When the last check finishes, the review opens on its own

Why it is built this way

The fixed rules always run first and the model sees text with those patterns already masked, exactly as on the command line. Checking with both models shows them side by side on the review screen, which is how the Oct 27 benchmark was read. Uploads are kept exactly as received so there is always an untouched original.

4Review

Laid out like Acrobat and the FOIA review tools. One toolbar across the top. Below it, the original on the left with every suggestion highlighted but still readable, and the redacted preview on the right showing what the requester would get. Under the preview, a list of every suggestion with who found it and why.

Each suggestion
Redact or Keep. "Redact all remaining" accepts whatever is still undecided.

Saving
Every click saves. The bottom bar says "All changes saved."

How the two panes read: a yellow mark is a suggestion not yet decided, a pink mark is one you confirmed. In the preview, a gray bar is undecided, a black bar is confirmed, and plain text is something you chose to keep.

Why it is built this way

The command-line engine replaces text as it goes. The web app instead keeps where each span sits in the record so it can be highlighted, approved or rejected one at a time, and the release file applies only the approved ones. A span a model returns that the app cannot find word for word in the record is dropped and noted, so nothing is redacted that was never there. Exemption labels on each suggestion are candidates for counsel, not determinations.

5Sign off

A summary of the whole request: records, redactions approved, suggestions kept visible, anything still to decide, and the Vault count. Type a reviewer name and press "Sign off and save the release."

Blocked until
Every record has been checked and every suggestion has a decision
Saves
One redacted .txt per email marked for release, plus one internal decision log marked "internal, do not release"
Afterward
Download links for each file, and "Reopen for changes" if something was missed

Why it is built this way

The named reviewer is the point of the whole tool. The decision log records who kept and who redacted what, with the model's reasoning beside it, so a later question from counsel or the requester has an answer. Email notice to the requester is not built yet because it needs the district mail relay, which comes with hosting.

The two models

The rig and Claude, and when each is used

The top bar shows which models are ready right now. A model that is offline is greyed out wherever you would choose it.

ModelWhat it isWorks fromBenchmark, Oct 27
RigThe district's own self-hosted language model. Record content stays inside the district network.The office network only. The VPN does not reach it today.Caught 66% of planted items. Missed attorney advice, employee medical details and special education status every time.
ClaudeAnthropic's hosted model through the official SDK. The app prints a note when record content goes to it.Anywhere with internet, if the API key is in the Mac's Keychain.Caught 99% of planted items.

The district's ruling of 2026-10-23: the self-hosted model is preferred, not required, for anything that reads student records. So the app defaults to the rig for redaction, and either model is fine for the search plan since a request holds no records. Which model should read real record content is still an open decision, and the benchmark numbers above are the evidence for that conversation.

The benchmark also showed the fixed rules hiding things they should not: the district's own public office phone numbers and known vendor email addresses. A reviewer has to Keep those by hand for now. Watch for it during testing.

The real app, on the Mac it runs on

The app listens only on that computer, so testing the live version means sitting at it or screen-sharing. From the project folder:

.venv/bin/python web/app.py

Then open http://localhost:8000. Ctrl+C in Terminal stops it. Claude is ready when ANTHROPIC_API_KEY is in the environment the app starts in; without it the app starts with the rig only. To use the rig you must be on the district network.

Test walkthrough

One fictional request, start to finish

This is the path to take the first time. It uses only the built-in samples and takes about fifteen minutes with Claude. Expect two waits of 30 to 40 seconds for the search plan and one per email for the redaction check. Write down anything that surprises you, even if it is not clearly a bug.

  1. Start. Open the home page. It lists existing requests with a status, or says "No requests yet." Press "New request."
  2. Fill the request. Press "Use the fictional sample request." The band trip request appears, asking for emails about moving a spring band trip from Anaheim to Flagstaff, the cost comparison shown to the board, and parent complaints. Enter a made-up name and email, keep today's date, choose Claude, tick the fictional box, and press "Create and draft the search plan."
  3. Read the plan. You should land on Step 2 with a new request number. Check that the summary matches the request, that the date range starts around the 2025-26 school year, and that the Gmail terms are one OR group of quoted phrases with no AND and no minus signs.
  4. Answer the question. The AI will probably suggest asking first, since the request leaves things open such as which text messages and what counts as the district office. Press "No, ask the requester first." Read the email. It should greet them properly, cite the request number and date, and number its questions. Try Copy message.
  5. Paste a reply. In "When they answer," paste a short made-up answer such as "Emails only, no texts. District office means the superintendent and CFO. Through October 2025 is fine." Press "Add their answer and redraft." The plan should come back narrower and the reply should appear under the request.
  6. Approve. Press "Yes, build the search." Try the Copy buttons and "Copy handoff for Technology." Then "Approve plan and continue." You should land on Step 3.
  7. Record the count. In box 1, type a Vault Count such as 340 and a note, press Save. A "Cost estimate for the requester" card appears with the AI cost, the page charge, and the email. Press "Mark as sent", then "Requester said proceed". In box 2, press "Add the 6 fictional sample emails instead." Six rows should appear in box 3, all "Not checked."
  8. Run the check. Choose "Both" if you are on the district network, otherwise Claude. Press "Run the redaction check (6)." Each row should update to a suggestion count. When the last one finishes the review opens on its own.
  9. Review the first email. Confirm the left pane shows yellow marks and the right pane shows gray bars in the same places. Press J a few times to move, A to redact one, R to keep one. Watch the preview change and the bottom bar say "All changes saved." Reload the page: the decisions must still be there.
  10. Add one by hand. Turn on "Redact text," pick a category, and highlight a word in the original. It should appear in the list as "Added by you." Press Esc. Try "Find a name or phrase" with a name from the email and "Redact all matches."
  11. Keep something the rules got wrong. Look for a district office phone number or a vendor email that the fixed rules hid. Press Keep on it. This is a known gap, so note whether you found one and where.
  12. Finish the rest. Press "Redact all remaining," then the green "Next email" button. Do the same for the remaining five. On the last one the button reads "Continue to sign off."
  13. Sign off. Check the summary numbers add up to what you decided. Leave the name blank and press sign off: it should refuse. Type a name and press "Sign off and save the release." You should see six files for release and one marked internal.
  14. Check the output. Download one released file and read it. Every approved redaction should be hidden and nothing you kept should be. Open the internal log and confirm it names you and lists every decision.
  15. Reopen. Press "Reopen for changes," change one decision, sign off again, and confirm the released file changed.

Things worth trying to break. Upload a .txt that is not an email. Paste an empty reply. Press Back in the browser during the 30-second wait. Open the same request in two tabs and decide in both. Remove a record after it was checked. Create a request without ticking the fictional box.

Reporting problems

How to say what you found

On the page itself

Every page of the app has a Feedback button from the district Site Feedback layer. Press it, click the part of the page you mean, and type what should change. Comments collect on the project's card at the district feedback board. This is the quickest way and it records exactly where you were.

What makes a note useful

  • The request number and which step
  • Which model you had chosen
  • What you expected and what happened instead
  • For a redaction miss or a wrong redaction: which sample email and the exact words
  • For something slow: roughly how long it took
